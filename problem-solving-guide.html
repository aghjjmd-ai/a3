The Problem Solving Guide

Most problems feel hard because they are vague. A simple, repeatable process turns a fog of worry into a list of next steps.

1

Define the problem

Write one sentence that says what is wrong, and one that says what “solved” would look like. “I am bad at maths” is a feeling. “I lose most marks on algebra questions and want to pass the test on the 20th” is a problem you can work on.

A problem well stated is half solved. If you cannot write the sentence, start by gathering facts until you can.

2

Find the cause, not the symptom

Ask “why?” several times in a row. Each answer becomes the next question.

  1. Why do I lose marks on algebra? I make mistakes rearranging equations.
  2. Why? I skip steps to save time.
  3. Why? I run out of time in tests.
  4. Why? I spend too long on the early questions.

The problem was never algebra. It was timing. Practising algebra would not have fixed it.

3

Generate options

Aim for several possible solutions before judging any of them. Quantity first, quality later. Useful prompts:

  • What would I tell a friend to do?
  • What would the opposite approach look like?
  • What has worked for a similar problem before?
  • What is the smallest thing that would help?
4

Choose with simple criteria

Score each option against two or three things that matter, such as cost, time and how likely it is to work.

OptionTime neededLikely to workEffort
Practise timed papersMediumHighMedium
Skip hard questions firstLowMediumLow
Extra algebra lessonsHighLowHigh

The best choice is rarely perfect. It is the one that is good enough and that you will actually do.

5

Test small, then scale

Try the chosen solution in a small way first. One timed paper, one conversation, one week of a new routine. Decide in advance how you will know whether it worked. Then keep it, change it or try the next option.

6

Working through it with others

Groups solve problems well when everyone speaks before anyone decides. Ask each person to write their ideas down first, then share. It stops the loudest voice setting the direction and reduces groupthink. Agree the definition of the problem before arguing about solutions; most disagreements are really about different problems.

7

When you are stuck

Change something. Explain the problem aloud to someone else, draw it, work backwards from the goal, or take a proper break. The Problem Breakdown tool suggests first steps for the most common kinds of stuck.
