The Critical Thinking Guide

Critical thinking is not about doubting everything. It is about matching how sure you are to how good the evidence is.

1

Start with the claim

Before you can judge an idea, you need to know exactly what it says. Rewrite it in one plain sentence. “This app improves memory” becomes: people who use this app for a stated period remember more on a stated test than people who do not. Suddenly it is clear what evidence would be needed, and what is missing.

Vague words such as “boosts”, “helps” and “linked to” are signs that a claim has not been pinned down.

2

Weigh the evidence

Not all evidence is equal. A rough order, from weaker to stronger:

  1. A personal story or a single example
  2. An expert’s opinion without data
  3. A survey or observation of many cases
  4. A controlled comparison between groups
  5. Many independent studies that agree

Stories are memorable and often true, but one person’s experience cannot tell you what usually happens.

3

Check the source

AskGood signWarning sign
Who wrote it?Named, with relevant knowledgeAnonymous or no relevant background
Where is the evidence?Links or references you can follow“Studies show” with no detail
When?Recent for fast-moving topicsOld and presented as new
Why?To informTo sell, recruit or provoke
Who else agrees?Independent sourcesOnly copies of the same story

4

Correlation and cause

When two things rise and fall together, it is tempting to think one causes the other. Often a third factor drives both, or the link is pure coincidence. Ice-cream sales and sunburn rise together, but the cause of both is sunny weather. Before accepting that A causes B, ask whether B might cause A, whether something else causes both, and whether the pattern holds in other places and times.

5

Common fallacies

  • Attacking the person: dismissing an argument because of who makes it.
  • Straw man: answering an exaggerated version of what someone said.
  • False choice: presenting two options when there are more.
  • Appeal to popularity: “everyone does it” as proof.
  • Slippery slope: claiming one step will inevitably lead to an extreme.
  • Appeal to authority: trusting a famous name outside their field.

Naming a fallacy is not the same as winning an argument. A conclusion supported by a bad argument might still be true; it simply has not been shown.

6

Your own biases

Everyone’s thinking takes shortcuts. The most important is confirmation bias: we notice and remember the evidence that agrees with us. The best defence is a habit: for any belief you care about, deliberately look for the strongest case against it, written by someone who disagrees.

7

Changing your mind well

Good thinkers change their minds, but not every time someone pushes. Update in proportion to the new evidence. A single strong study should shift you a little; a pattern of strong studies should shift you a lot. Saying “I was wrong about that” is a sign of a working mind, not a weak one.
